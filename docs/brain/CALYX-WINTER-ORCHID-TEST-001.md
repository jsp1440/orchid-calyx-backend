# CALYX-WINTER-ORCHID-TEST-001

## Purpose

Acceptance test for Calyx federated scientific context: canonical Orchid Continuum graph context, local indexed evidence, Europe PMC external literature fallback, NOAA CPC seasonal climate context, and explicit evidence/inference boundaries.

## Test prompt

I grow orchids on the central California coast near Los Osos, where winters are cool and can be rainy. Please evaluate how a wetter-than-normal winter, including an El Niño-influenced winter if that is what the current NOAA Climate Prediction Center outlook supports, could affect orchids grown outdoors or in a cool greenhouse here.

Compare at least these groups: Cymbidium, Cattleya/Laelia alliance, Dendrobium, Sarcochilus, Phalaenopsis, Paphiopedilum, and Phragmipedium. Add other commonly grown orchid groups when the evidence suggests they would respond differently enough to be useful.

For Dendrobium, do not treat the genus as having one cultural requirement. Separate major horticultural/physiological groups where possible and identify which groups have evidence for a cool and/or dry winter rest, which do not, and what can happen if winter-rest plants remain warm and wet. Specifically investigate the hypothesis that failure to receive an appropriate cool/dry rest can favor continued vegetative growth or keiki production rather than floral induction in some Dendrobium groups. Explain what is actually supported about temperature, water status, photoperiod, carbohydrate status, and plant hormones; distinguish demonstrated mechanisms from horticultural interpretation.

For Sarcochilus, investigate whether cool winter exposure is an important floral-induction cue and whether cool conditions may matter more than winter dryness for commonly cultivated species and hybrids. Treat the observation that outdoor plants flower more strongly than warm-greenhouse plants as a hypothesis to compare with published evidence rather than as established fact.

For every group, distinguish at least: tolerance of winter rain or prolonged wetness; root/crown/rot risk; minimum-temperature sensitivity; whether a cool period promotes flowering; whether a dry rest is required, beneficial, neutral, or harmful; likely flowering consequences of keeping the plant too warm or too wet; and practical implications for outdoor culture on the central California coast.

Use the Orchid Continuum first. Consult current NOAA/NWS Climate Prediction Center seasonal information for the climate premise, and search the scientific literature for physiological and horticultural evidence. If local indexed literature coverage is incomplete, use the external literature discovery path and clearly label those papers as external/review-required rather than canonical Continuum evidence.

Present the main comparison as a compact table, followed by short explanatory sections for Dendrobium and Sarcochilus because their flowering cues are the main physiological test cases. Include citations or source identifiers wherever available. State evidence strength and uncertainty. Do not generalize from one species to an entire genus when the genus is physiologically diverse, and do not turn a climate forecast into evidence for orchid physiology.
